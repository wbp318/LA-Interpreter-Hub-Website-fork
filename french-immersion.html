Research brief

French immersion in Louisiana’s public schools.

What exists today, how it is staffed and funded, where the gaps are, and what expansion would take. Researched October 28, 2026.

Summary

The short version

  • Scale: CODOFIL reports 36 immersion schools with more than 5,500 students (undated). Other reports put it at 40+ schools in 14 parishes. Enrollment has grown from roughly 400 students in the early 1990s.
  • Staffing is the pressure point. Many classroom teachers come from abroad on CODOFIL-sponsored J-1 visas, and the home-grown pipeline is small.
  • Funding is fragile. A 2025 reversal at a Lafayette Parish pre-K program shows how a single district budget decision can cut a program.
  • Language variety is unsettled. Most programs teach standard French; some, like École Pointe-au-Chien, center local Louisiana French.
  • Open item: I could not confirm the final outcome of the 2026 bills HB 350 and SB 327. Check the Legislature’s site before citing them.

Teachers

The staffing pipeline

International associate teachers come mainly from France, plus Belgium, Canada, Cameroon, Senegal and Ivory Coast. They work on J-1 visas that CODOFIL sponsors, valid for three years and extendable by two. France renewed its agreement with Louisiana in October 2024 for another four years. The domestic route, Escadrille Louisiane, helps people become certified French teachers, but sources suggest it cannot yet fill the need. A University of Louisiana at Lafayette proposal for a dedicated immersion-teacher degree also appeared in search results; I did not confirm its status.

Text version of this chart
  1. International route: apply through a home-country body (France Éducation International or Wallonie-Bruxelles International), CODOFIL selects and places, then a J-1 visa for 3 years, extendable by 2.
  2. Domestic route: Escadrille Louisiane leads to Louisiana teacher certification.
  3. Both routes feed immersion classrooms. Where the gap remains, schools lean on international recruitment; building the domestic route is the longer-term fix.
Funding and policy

What I could and could not confirm

Confirmed

  • HB 350 (2026), École Pointe-au-Chien: would raise the school’s top grade from 4 to 8. It passed the House 98 to 0 on April 27, was reported favorably by Senate Education on May 21, and was recommitted to Senate Finance on May 25. The page I read showed no governor action or Act number. See the Legislature’s bill page Read at source
  • Lafayette Parish: the district announced cuts to pre-K immersion at Myrtle Place Elementary, then reinstated it for 2025-26 after pushback Reported

Not confirmed

  • SB 327 (2026): would amend the immersion statute and name a historical French Bible as the state book. Trackers show only a March committee referral, and one tracker’s “Became Law” label looked unreliable. Treat its status as unknown.
  • 2025 funding or BESE action: I found no confirmed 2025 appropriation or BESE decision on immersion.
  • Per-teacher state support: a 2024-era fiscal note describes a $21,000 per-teacher allocation, a 300-teacher cap, and a 25-parent trigger. I could not confirm that this became law, so do not cite it as current policy.
Language variety

Standard French or Louisiana French?

Sources describe a middle path. A study of East Baton Rouge immersion teachers found they favored standard French as the main instructional variety, with some Louisiana French included. CODOFIL reportedly advises international teachers not to tell students they are wrong when they use heritage words. École Pointe-au-Chien deliberately centers the local dialect, for example teaching both the standard crevette and the local chevrette for shrimp. Scholars still debate how Louisiana French should be standardized and taught. Any proposal should say which variety it will teach and who reviews the materials.

Expansion

Levers for growth

My analysis from the evidence above, not a finding from the sources.

Text version of this chart
  • Teachers: international recruitment, domestic certification, immersion method training.
  • Funding: stable district budgets, state support.
  • Access: lotteries and open seats, parishes beyond South Louisiana.
  • Curriculum: standard and Louisiana French, local culture and heritage.
  • Policy: statute and BESE rules, partner agreements.
  • Teacher pipeline first. Every other lever depends on classroom staff. Reported gaps include no formal immersion training and no chance to observe immersion classes.
  • Protect existing programs. The Myrtle Place episode shows that district-level cuts can undo gains, so multi-year commitments matter.
  • Go where programs are thin. Most programs are in South Louisiana; Caddo and Lincoln show interest elsewhere.
  • Decide the language question early. The choice between standard and Louisiana French changes who you recruit, what you buy, and who reviews materials.
